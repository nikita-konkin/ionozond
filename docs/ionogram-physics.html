Part 0

The chain, end to end

Six stages sit between the transmitter and a pixel. Every feature you see on an ionogram — real or spurious — is introduced at exactly one of them, and knowing which is most of diagnosis.

  1. Transmit. A linear FM sweep, 7.5 → 32.5 MHz at a known rate, starting on a known UTC second.
  2. Propagate. The ionosphere refracts some frequencies back to earth and lets others through. This stage creates the trace and decides where it ends.
  3. Receive. 25 MS/s of complex samples, timestamped against GPS.
  4. Dechirp. Multiply by a conjugate replica of the sweep. Delay becomes a constant beat frequency.
  5. Transform. Decimate, then one FFT per 0.41 s of sweep. Beat frequency becomes a row; sweep time becomes a column.
  6. Gate and render. Normalise each spectrum, threshold it, remove speckle, map to colour.

Stage 2 is the measurement. Stages 3–6 are supposed to be transparent, and the interesting failures are the ones where they are not.

Part 1 · Physics

Why the ionosphere reflects at all

Free electrons make the upper atmosphere a dispersive plasma. Each height has a characteristic frequency; below it, waves turn back.

The plasma frequency of a region with electron density Ne is

fp ≈ 8.98 √Ne   [kHz]

Ne in electrons per cm³. An F2 peak of 106 cm⁻³ gives 8.98 MHz; 1.5 × 106 gives 11.0 MHz. This is the whole reason ionosondes work in the HF band and nowhere else.

The refractive index of that plasma, ignoring the magnetic field and collisions, is

n = √(1 − fp² ⁄ f²)

A wave sent straight up turns around where n = 0, that is where f = fp. Send it at a frequency above the layer's peak plasma frequency and it never finds such a height: it goes through and out. That threshold, measured vertically, is the layer's critical frequency, written foF2 for the F2 layer.

Two consequences matter more than anything else in this document. First, the critical frequency tracks electron density, which tracks solar illumination — so everything about an ionogram changes between day and night. Second, n < 1 inside the layer, and group velocity is c · n, so a wave that penetrates deeply is slowed. That slowing is what bends the trace upward near its high-frequency end.

Part 2 · Physics

Oblique geometry, and why 2634 km buys you 30 MHz

A vertical sounder working the same ionosphere would stop at 10 MHz. Shooting along the ground rather than up at it multiplies the usable frequency by roughly three.

A ray striking the layer at incidence angle φ reflects when its vertical component reaches the plasma frequency. Rearranged, that is the secant law:

foblique = fvertical · sec φ

Flat-earth form. Over 2634 km with a 300 km reflection height the geometry gives sec φ = 4.5, but earth curvature makes that an overestimate; the curved-earth factor for this path is nearer 3. With foF2 around 10 MHz that predicts a maximum usable frequency near 30 MHz — which is where the daytime trace on this circuit actually stops.

The delay the receiver measures is the group path P′ — the distance light would cover in the observed time — not the true ray path, because the wave travels slower than c inside the layer. Treating the reflection as a mirror at virtual height h′ makes the arithmetic elementary:

P′ = c · τ
P′ = 2 nhop √( (D ⁄ 2nhop)² + h′² )

D is ground distance, nhop the number of ionospheric reflections. The mirror is a fiction — the wave really turns gradually below h′ — but it reproduces the measured delay exactly, which is all the axis needs.

Three things fall out of this table that are worth internalising.

Mode spacing identifies the mode. Traces separated by 0.6–1.6 ms on this path are hop orders. Anything separated by very much more is not.

The 1-hop delay barely moves with layer height — 0.15 ms across a 100 km change — while the 3-hop delay moves 1.1 ms. Higher-order modes are the sensitive ones, so if you want to watch the layer rise and fall through the evening, watch 3F, not 1F.

Higher hop orders have steeper take-off angles, hence smaller secant factors, hence lower MUFs. The 1-hop trace always extends furthest in frequency; 2F and 3F stop short of it. When you see several traces ending at different frequencies, that ordering is the confirmation they are hop modes.

Why E-layer modes are absent here

A single hop off the E layer at 110 km cannot span much beyond 2000 km — the geometry runs out of elevation angle. At 2634 km any E-layer path needs two hops or more, which is why the traces on this circuit are F-layer modes and sporadic-E, when it appears, shows up as a short-delay intruder rather than the main event.

Part 4 · Physics

Where the trace begins and ends

Both ends of an ionogram trace are physical measurements. The high end is penetration, the low end is absorption, and they move on completely different schedules.

The high-frequency end: MUF and the nose

As the sweep approaches the maximum usable frequency, the ray penetrates deeper before turning, spends longer in the region where n < 1, and is delayed more. The trace therefore curves upward in delay before it stops — the characteristic nose. Past the MUF nothing returns at all.

Diagnostic worth memorising

A genuine MUF cut-off always has a nose. A trace that runs flat and stops at a hard vertical edge is the instrument, not the ionosphere. At a different frequency each capture, it is lost samples; that distinction separated a propagation question from a dropped-packet bug on this station. At the same frequency every time, it is just the end of the recording window (Part 7).

The low-frequency end: LUF

Below the MUF the limit is absorption, not reflection. The D region at 60–90 km absorbs in proportion to roughly f−2, so the lowest usable frequency rises sharply in daylight and collapses at night when the D region disappears. On a 250 s sweep starting at 7.5 MHz the daytime LUF is often below the start of the sweep, which means the instrument cannot see it — the trace simply begins at the left edge.

The magneto-ionic split

The geomagnetic field makes the plasma birefringent, so each ray propagates as two modes — ordinary and extraordinary — with slightly different refractive indices. They reflect at slightly different heights and appear as two closely spaced traces, separated by about half the electron gyrofrequency (≈ 0.7 MHz) in equivalent vertical terms. Seeing the split is a sign of a clean capture; losing it to the speckle filter is a sign the gate is too tight.

Part 5 · Instrument

The dechirp: how delay becomes frequency

The single idea the whole receiver rests on. A sweep multiplied by a copy of itself turns time-of-flight into a tone you can measure with an FFT.

The transmitter emits a linear sweep. The receiver generates the same sweep locally and multiplies the incoming signal by its conjugate:

y[n] = x[n] · conj( exp( 2πi ( f0t + ½ k t² ) ) ),  t = n ⁄ fs

An echo arriving τ late carries the frequency the transmitter had τ ago. Since the replica has moved on by k·τ in that time, the difference is constant for the whole sweep:

beat = −k · τ

k = 100 kHz/s is the sweep rate. Negative because a late arrival sits below the replica, which is why the console reverses its spectra so rows ascend with delay. Every echo becomes a steady tone; one FFT resolves them all at once.

Part 6 · Instrument

From sweep to pixel

The two axes come from two different places: the horizontal from a clock, the vertical from an FFT.

Horizontal (frequency). The transmitter's frequency is a known function of time since the sweep began, so a column's frequency is read off the clock: f = 7.5 MHz + k·t. Nothing in the received signal identifies the frequency — the axis is entirely an assertion about timing.

Vertical (delay, or range). Each FFT bin is a beat frequency, converted by τ = −beat/k and shown as either time or group path c·τ.

FFT length is a pure trade: doubling it halves the frequency step and halves the delay bin in the other direction. 16 384 is where the two agree — the transmitter moves 41 kHz during the 0.41 s each spectrum integrates, so neither axis is being resolved finer than the other can support.

A finer picture is not a finer measurement

The picture is drawn by a separate pass: each FFT is zero-padded four times over, and two overlapping windows are taken per spectrum (Parameters → детализация по задержке / по частоте, ×4 / ×2 by default). Nothing in the table changes. A trace is still one 7.32 km bin tall and 41 kHz of sweep wide. What changes is that the picture interpolates between bins instead of drawing a staircase of them, so two features a bin or two apart — a nose and the layer below it, an O/X pair — stop merging into one block.

Only the picture changes. LUF, MUF, the SNR and ПЗМ panels and the .h5 archive are computed on the analysis grid above, and stay bit-for-bit identical at any setting.

Part 7 · Operating

The recording window: rep, chirptime, duration

The transmitter always sweeps its whole band. The schedule only decides which slice of that sweep the receiver listens to, and a slice is still a complete ionogram, just a narrower one.

Why NIC4 still shows a trace at 105 s

Each column of an ionogram is 0.41 s of sweep and stands on its own. Nothing in the column at 12 MHz depends on the one at 25 MHz. Recording 105 s instead of 250 s keeps the first 105 s of the sweep, 7.5 to 18.0 MHz, and drops 18–32.5 MHz. The echo delay, the dechirp and the timing are exactly as before.

What is lost is the top of the band. At night the 1F MUF on this path is roughly 11–18 MHz, so nothing is lost. By day the trace runs on towards 25–32 MHz, and its upper part, nose included, is cut off at 18 MHz.

And the 280 in NIC4_280 is not a duration. It is a transmitter timing label, 300:280 in the notation of Part 9. As that part shows, the station of that name actually receives a different Cyprus sweep.

The receiver sees a fixed 25 MHz slice of the spectrum, centred on the local oscillator. The transmitter crosses that slice once per sweep, in a straight line. A capture is the stretch of that line between chirptime and chirptime + dur:

flow = LO − fs ⁄ 2     fhigh = flow + k · dur
dur ≤ fs ⁄ k

fs = 25 MHz, k the sweep rate. At 100 kHz/s the whole window takes 250 s; at 500 kHz/s it takes 50 s, which is why Sodankylä's duration is 50. That is its entire window, not a compromise. A duration longer than fs/k records the transmitter after it has left the window, which is nothing.

Part 8 · Operating

In turn or in parallel

In the air, parallel is normal: several transmitters sweep at once, and the dechirp separates them by timing alone. In this receiver, soundings run one at a time, and that is a choice about where the dechirp happens, not a law of physics.

Many transmitters, one band, no confusion

Cyprus runs sweeps at 300:235, 300:240 and 300:245, each 5 s behind the last and therefore 500 kHz lower at every instant. All three are inside the receiver's window at once. Dechirping against the 235 replica puts the 240 sweep at a beat of 500 kHz and the 245 sweep at 1 MHz. The decimator is a 625-sample boxcar, with nulls every 40 kHz. It passes only what lies within ±20 kHz of the replica and rejects the rest: 500 kHz comes out 32 dB down, and 1 MHz lands exactly on a null.

So a transmitter is selected by when the replica starts, not by where the receiver is tuned. It is the same arithmetic that makes a wrong chirptime lose the signal (Part 9), seen from the other side.

One receiver, one sounding at a time

The sounder dechirps live, at 25 MS/s, and keeps only the 40 kHz product of one replica. Nothing else from the 25 MHz is stored. It can therefore follow one transmitter per capture, and the scheduler is correspondingly simple:

  • For every active station it computes the next start at least 3 s ahead, and records whichever comes first.
  • That capture runs its full duration. Then up to about a second of product building (.lfp, .h5), then it looks again.
  • A station whose start fell inside that capture is simply no longer due; its next start is one rep later. If the same overlap happens every cycle, that station is never recorded, and nothing in the log says so.
  • Settings reach the sounder only when it starts. After editing the schedule or parameters, press STOP and then START.

What real parallel sounding would take

  • One stream, several replicas. The 25 MS/s stream already contains every transmitter in the window. Dechirping it against two replicas at once gives two soundings from one capture, with no scheduling conflict. The cost is a second multiply-and-decimate at 25 MS/s. That doubles the load of the part of the sounder that has to keep up with the radio. It also only works for stations inside the same 25 MHz window. A common LO of 16 MHz would hold both of ours, since Cyprus's trace starts near 8 MHz and Sodankylä's near 4, but each station has its own LO today. Not implemented.
  • Keep the raw stream. chirpsounder2 records the full 25 MS/s into a RAM ring buffer, 100 MB every second, and dechirps any number of sounders from it after the fact. That is how it detects transmitters it was never told about. The price is gigabytes of RAM and CPU to match.
  • More receivers. The original dsChirp's configuration is a list of lists of sounders, one list per receiver thread. That format survives in chirp_config.py. Parallel, as its author meant it, was one radio per list.

Within a 300 s cycle, in-turn scheduling is rarely the limit. The two stations use about half of it. The real constraint is that two transmitters whose best windows overlap cannot both be followed. Then one must move to another of its own sweeps, which is why Sodankylä, sweeping every minute, can simply take minute 2.

Part 9 · Operating

Finding a sounder's timing

A sounder is identified by its sweep rate and its timing. Get either wrong by more than a few milliseconds and the capture is empty. Sometimes it is worse than empty: it looks like a trace in the wrong place.

Chirp lists, Twente's among them, give timing as rep:offset, the G3PLX notation. rep is the period. offset is the second, after each full hour, at which the sweep passes 0 MHz. The schedule wants the moment the sweep reaches the bottom of this receiver's window, so the conversion adds the time the sweep takes to get there:

chirptime = ( offset + (LO − fs⁄2) ⁄ k ) mod rep

When the station's rep is a multiple of the transmitter's, add whole transmitter periods to choose which of its sweeps to take. Sodankylä sweeps every 60 s and the schedule takes one in five; every minute is equally valid, so pick the one that does not collide.

What a wrong chirptime does

A timing error Δt shifts the replica by k·Δt of frequency, so it looks exactly like extra delay. Small errors move the trace: 1 ms is 300 km. Larger errors wrap around the unambiguous range, and the boxcar decimator attenuates them. Whole seconds either sit on the wrap or vanish into a null:

Part 12 · Reading

White stripes in the daily-course panels

The signal-to-noise and power-delay panels are built one column per capture, so anything that costs a capture leaves a white column. The shape of the white says which thing went wrong — these panels are a fault log as much as a propagation record.

Read the striping before reading the colour. A day of continuous colour is evidence the receive path is healthy, which is worth more than any single ionogram looking good.
StripeMeaningStage
Full height, one column A sounding produced no usable file — a .partial from a failed capture. receive
Full height, several adjacent columns The loop stopped and was restarted. The run guard gives up after three empty soundings, so a block of them is its signature rather than a coincidence. receive
Upper part only, ragged lower edge The capture completed but the trace was cut mid-sweep. Above the cut there is no signal to report, so the column ends where the trace did. receive
Speckled white through the body Gate too strict — individual spectra with nothing surviving. Not a capture problem. gate
Broad white region, smooth edges, follows the clock Propagation. The band closing at dusk is a shape, not a stripe. propagate
A whole day, read this way

30 August 2026 on this circuit: dense striping until roughly 17:30 and none afterwards. Three fixes landed in that hour — the NIC receive ring raised from 256 to 4096 entries, rmem_max from 50 to 100 MB, and 4000-byte UDP frames — and every capture since has been clean.

Before them, the ragged upper edges were overflows cutting the trace mid-sweep, and the full-height blocks were the guard stopping a loop that had produced three empty soundings in a row. Neither was propagation, and neither would have been visible from a single ionogram.

The asymmetry that matters

A lost sample is not a lost sample — it is a shifted time base. Because the dechirp derives frequency from a sample count, any gap silently relabels every subsequent sample and moves the entire remaining trace by the duration of the gap. Verified against a synthetic echo: 1 ms lost moved it exactly 300 km, 40 ms moved it 12 000 km, and zero-filling the gap restored the beat to its original value to within 0.1 Hz.

This is why the receiver pads gaps rather than merely counting them, and why a capture reporting overflows can still be perfectly usable.

The speckle gate, quantified

The final stage keeps a point only if enough neighbours in a w × h window survived. A trace occupying k delay rows contributes at most w·k neighbours, so the threshold does not fade — it steps:

rows required = ⌈ level ⁄ w ⌉

With the 9 × 3 default, any level above 9 deletes a one-row-thick trace outright however strong it is; above 27 nothing survives at all. Between the steps the threshold only trades noise: a clean two-row trace survived every level from 6 to 14 in testing while noise fell from 96.9% to 14.3%.

Part 14

What this instrument cannot currently measure

Stated plainly so nobody builds on it by accident.

The reported LUF and MUF are not measurements. Across all 288 captures of one real day they read 7.66 and 32.34 MHz — the two ends of the sweep — in almost every one, with no diurnal variation on a circuit where the true MUF is known to swing from about 11 MHz at night to 32 by mid-morning. The extractor asks only for three consecutive spectra each holding three consecutive surviving points, which band-edge interference satisfies trivially. Profiling confirms it: on a capture whose visible trace ends near 20 MHz, the trace band held 3–7 surviving points per spectrum while the 23–31 MHz band held 12–31. The trace is a minority of what survives the gate.

A working extractor has to require delay continuity across adjacent spectra — the trace is a connected curve, and that is precisely the property the present test ignores. Until then, read the picture, not the numbers.

Absolute range depends on the clock being GPS-locked. When the GPSDO drops out, the fallback keeps the sounding running but the delay axis becomes NTP-grade — hundreds to thousands of kilometres of possible offset. Captures taken unlocked are fine for studying structure and worthless for absolute height.

Nothing here measures electron density directly. Everything above is group path against frequency. Turning that into a density profile requires inversion — solving for Ne(h) from the retardation encoded in the trace shape — and this station does not attempt it.
